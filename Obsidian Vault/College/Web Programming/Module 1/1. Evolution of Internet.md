## 1. Introduction to the Internet

The Internet is the world’s largest interconnection of computer networks, enabling global communication and information exchange. It is a packet-switched, TCP/IP-based network that connects millions of private, public, academic, commercial, and government networks.

The term “Internet” stands for “INTERconnected NETwork”, emphasizing its structure: smaller networks cooperating to form one global network.

The fundamental goals of the Internet include resource sharing, distributed computing, and robust communication across geographically separated hosts.

---

## 2. Early Development: ARPANET

The origin of the Internet can be traced to ARPANET, a project funded by the Advanced Research Projects Agency (ARPA) of the U.S. Department of Defense in the late 1960s.

ARPANET was conceived to:

- Create a decentralized communication system.
    
- Ensure communication remains functional even if parts of the network are damaged.
    
- Support resource sharing between research institutions.
    

Key characteristics:

1. First wide-area packet-switched network.
    
2. Connected major research institutions.
    
3. Demonstrated survivability and redundancy through distributed architecture.
    

ARPANET initially connected four major universities and later expanded significantly, becoming the testing ground for networking protocols and technologies.

---

## 3. Packet Switching

Packet switching is a fundamental concept that revolutionized digital communication. Instead of sending data as a continuous stream, packet switching divides the message into smaller units called packets.

Each packet contains:

- Source and destination addresses.
    
- Sequence information.
    
- Error detection and correction metadata.
    
- A portion of the actual message data.
    

Advantages of packet switching include:

- Efficient bandwidth utilization.
    
- Ability to reroute packets dynamically during network failures.
    
- No need for dedicated circuits, unlike circuit switching.
    
- Improved reliability and robustness.
    

Packet switching ensured that the ARPANET could continue functioning even if multiple nodes failed, thus meeting the original military requirement for survivability.

This concept later became the foundation of modern computer networking.

---

## 4. Expansion: BITNET and CSNET

During the late 1970s and early 1980s, two significant academic networks were developed:

1. BITNET (Because It’s Time Network):  
    Provided email and file transfer services to universities and research institutions.
    
2. CSNET (Computer Science Network):  
    Enabled institutions without ARPANET access to communicate and collaborate.
    

These networks expanded digital communication beyond military and research contexts, helping universities and scientific communities adopt networking technologies.

---

## 5. NSFNET and the Emergence of the Modern Internet

In 1986, the National Science Foundation launched NSFNET, a high-speed backbone connecting major supercomputing centers in the United States.

Important contributions of NSFNET:

- Created a large-scale academic and research network.
    
- Provided high-speed data transfer between universities.
    
- Replaced ARPANET as the primary backbone by 1990.
    
- Operated at increasingly higher speeds (from 56 kbps to T3 lines).
    

By the mid-1990s, NSFNET transitioned into commercial Internet Service Providers (ISPs), and the modern Internet emerged as a global, publicly accessible network.

---

## 6. TCP/IP Protocol Suite

The Transmission Control Protocol/Internet Protocol (TCP/IP) is the fundamental communication protocol suite of the Internet.

TCP responsibilities:

- Break messages into packets at the source.
    
- Ensure reliable, ordered delivery.
    
- Detect and correct transmission errors.
    
- Reassemble packets at the destination.
    

IP responsibilities:

- Route packets from source to destination.
    
- Assign logical addressing (IP addresses).
    
- Execute best-effort packet delivery across interconnected networks.
    

Together, TCP and IP enable seamless end-to-end communication, regardless of the hardware or operating system of the participating machines.

Every Internet-connected device is assigned an IP address and communicates using TCP/IP standards.

---

## 7. IP Addressing: IPv4 and IPv6

IP addressing identifies hosts on the Internet.

### 7.1 IPv4

- 32-bit address.
    
- Represented as four decimal numbers separated by dots (e.g., 192.168.1.10).
    
- Approximately 4.3 billion possible addresses.
    
- Address exhaustion occurred due to rapid Internet expansion.
    

### 7.2 IPv6

- 128-bit address.
    
- Represented in eight groups of hexadecimal numbers separated by colons.
    
- Provides a vastly larger address space.
    
- Enhances security and routing efficiency.
    
- Designed to gradually replace IPv4.

## 8. Domain Names and DNS

IP addresses are difficult for humans to remember. To simplify this, domain names were introduced.

A domain name such as  
[www.cars.maruthi.org](http://www.cars.maruthi.org/)  
consists of:

- Host name: www
    
- Subdomain: cars
    
- Domain name: maruthi
    
- Top-Level Domain (TLD): org
    

DNS (Domain Name System) is used to translate domain names into their corresponding IP addresses.  
It functions as a distributed, hierarchical naming system, enabling users to access websites without memorizing numeric IP addresses.

DNS is essential because almost all applications rely on domain name resolution for accessing web resources.

---

## 9. World Wide Web (WWW)

The World Wide Web was introduced by Tim Berners-Lee in 1989 at CERN as a system of interlinked hypertext documents.

Key components introduced by the WWW:

1. HTML (HyperText Markup Language).
    
2. HTTP (HyperText Transfer Protocol).
    
3. URL (Uniform Resource Locator).
    
4. Hyperlinks to navigate documents.
    
5. Browser-based rendering of multimedia content.
    

The WWW operates as a service on top of the underlying Internet infrastructure. The Internet is the physical and logical network, whereas the WWW is the collection of multimedia documents accessible over that network.

---

## 10. HTTP and HTTPS

HTTP is an application-layer protocol that defines how messages are formatted and transmitted between clients (browsers) and servers.

Characteristics of HTTP:

- Stateless protocol (each request is independent).
    
- Uses methods such as GET, POST, PUT, DELETE.
    
- Transfers HTML pages, images, scripts, and other web resources.
    

HTTPS is the secure version of HTTP:

- Uses SSL/TLS encryption.
    
- Protects data integrity, authentication, and confidentiality.
    
- Essential for online banking, e-commerce, and data-sensitive services.
