- In **K-Means**, the "captain" is an imaginary average coordinate (a ghost).
    
- In **K-Medoids (PAM)**, the "captain" (the Medoid) must be an _actual, physical data point_ from your dataset.
    

Here is the crystal-clear breakdown of the algorithm, the 4 cases, and the pros/cons.

---

## Part 1: The PAM Algorithm Steps (Simplified)

PAM stands for **Partitioning Around Medoids**. Its entire goal is to test different data points as "captains" until it finds the combination that results in the tightest, most accurate teams (clusters).

Here is the exact algorithmic flow:

1. **Initial Selection:** Arbitrarily choose $k$ actual objects from your dataset to be the initial representative objects (the first medoids/captains).
    
2. **Form Teams:** Assign every other remaining data object to the cluster of the medoid it is closest to.
    
3. **Pick a Challenger:** Randomly select a normal, non-representative object and call it $O_{random}$ (the challenger).
    
4. **Calculate the Swap Cost:** Mathematically test what would happen if you fired a current medoid ($O_j$) and replaced them with the challenger ($O_{random}$). You calculate the total cost $S$ (the change in distance for all points).
    
5. **Make the Swap (if better):** If the total cost $S < 0$ (meaning the overall distances shrank and the clusters got tighter), you officially swap $O_j$ with $O_{random}$. $O_{random}$ is now a new medoid!
    
6. **Repeat:** Repeat steps 2 through 5 until there are absolutely no more changes (the clusters are fully optimized).
    

---

## Part 2: The 4 Swapping Cases Explained

Step 4 of the algorithm is the hardest. When PAM tests swapping out an old medoid ($O_j$) for the new challenger ($O_{random}$), every single normal data point (let's call a normal point **$p$**) has to re-evaluate who its closest captain is.

There are exactly 4 possible cases for point $p$:

**Scenario A: Point $p$ originally belonged to the old medoid ($O_j$) that is getting fired.**

- **Case 1 (Reassigned to another old captain):** $O_j$ is replaced by $O_{random}$. Point $p$ looks around and realizes that some _other_ existing medoid ($O_i$) is actually closer to it than the new guy ($O_{random}$). So, $p$ is reassigned to $O_i$.
    
- **Case 2 (Reassigned to the new captain):** $O_j$ is replaced by $O_{random}$. Point $p$ looks around and realizes the new guy ($O_{random}$) is closest. So, $p$ is reassigned to $O_{random}$.
    

**Scenario B: Point $p$ originally belonged to a completely different medoid ($O_i$).**

- **Case 3 (Nothing changes):** $O_j$ is replaced by $O_{random}$. Point $p$ looks at the new guy but realizes its current captain ($O_i$) is _still_ the closest. The assignment does not change.
    
- **Case 4 (Reassigned to the new captain):** $O_j$ is replaced by $O_{random}$. Point $p$ looks at the new guy and realizes $O_{random}$ is actually closer than its current captain ($O_i$). So, $p$ abandons $O_i$ and is reassigned to $O_{random}$.
    

To calculate the total cost $S$ of the swap, the algorithm adds up the distance changes for every single point $p$ across all 4 cases.

## Part 3: The "Rest" (Advantages, Disadvantages, and Complexity)

If an examiner asks you to evaluate PAM, you need to provide these three points:

- **The Big Advantage:** PAM is much more robust than K-Means in the presence of noise and outliers. Because it uses actual data points as centers, a single crazy outlier won't drag the cluster center into empty space.
    
- **The Big Disadvantage:** The process is computationally costly. It has to constantly test swapping pairs of points. Furthermore, just like K-Means, you still have to manually specify the number of clusters $k$ in advance.
    
- **The Complexity:** The computational complexity for each iteration is $O(k(n-k)^2)$, where $n$ is the total number of objects and $k$ is the number of clusters. Because of this heavy math, PAM is only useful for small datasets
