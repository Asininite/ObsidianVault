# **1. Criteria for Selecting a Wind Farm Site (Extremely Detailed)**

Selecting a proper wind farm site is crucial because the **energy in wind is proportional to the cube of wind speed**.  
A site with slightly higher wind can produce dramatically more power, so the site must be chosen using very careful technical, environmental, and economic evaluation.

Below are the most important criteria explained in depth:

---

## **A. Wind Resource Quality**

### **1. High Average Wind Speed**

- A wind farm becomes economical only when the **annual mean wind speed is usually ≥ 6 m/s** at hub height.
    
- Higher wind speeds increase energy yield disproportionately due to the cubic relationship (P ∝ V³).
    

### **2. Consistent Wind Direction**

- Sites with **stable wind direction** reduce yaw movements, improving mechanical life of the turbine and reducing energy losses.
    

### **3. Low Turbulence**

- Turbulence increases mechanical stress, structural vibration, fatigue loading, and reduces turbine lifetime.
    
- Open plains, sea shores, ridges, and smooth terrain produce low turbulence.
    

---

## **B. Topography / Terrain**

### **1. Elevated Locations**

- Hilltops, ridges, and high-altitude plains receive stronger winds due to wind acceleration over land features.
    

### **2. Absence of Obstacles**

- Buildings, forests, or mountains upwind cause wake effects and turbulence.
    
- “Wind shadow” regions reduce effective wind speed.
    

### **3. Smooth Surface Roughness**

- Areas with minimal ground friction (coastlines, deserts, grasslands) have higher wind speeds.
    

---

## **C. Geotechnical and Soil Conditions**

### **1. Strong Foundations**

- Turbines exert huge dynamic loads; hence soil must support deep reinforced concrete foundations.
    

### **2. Flood Resistance**

- Sites should be safe from water-logging, flooding, or erosion that could undermine foundations.
    

---

## **D. Accessibility and Infrastructure**

### **1. Road Transport**

- Turbine blades (up to 60–90 m long) and tower segments are enormous.
    
- Site must allow trucks, cranes, and heavy equipment to reach safely.
    

### **2. Electrical Grid Connectivity**

- Must be near transmission lines or substations.
    
- Grid must have the capacity to absorb generated power.
    

### **3. Maintenance Facilities**

- Access for regular servicing, lubrication, gearbox maintenance, etc.
    

---

## **E. Environmental and Social Factors**

### **1. Wildlife Protection**

- Avoid migratory bird routes, bird nesting areas, and bat habitats.
    

### **2. Noise Restrictions**

- Maintain safe distances from human settlements.
    

### **3. Land Use**

- Sufficient land area with minimal conflict (farmland is acceptable since turbines occupy only small patches).
    

### **4. Community Acceptance**

- Local population should agree to land leasing and turbine operations.
    

---

# **2. Define Pitch Control and Explain its Use (Very Detailed)**

Pitch control refers to the **mechanism that rotates wind turbine blades around their longitudinal axis** (twisting them) to control how much aerodynamic lift they generate.

---

## **Definition**

Pitch control is the system that **adjusts the angle of attack of wind turbine blades** to regulate the power extracted from wind and protect the turbine under high wind conditions.

---

## **Why Pitch Control Is Needed (Detailed Explanation)**

### **1. Power Regulation**

Above the rated wind speed (usually 11–13 m/s), wind would try to drive the turbine too fast.  
Pitch control reduces the aerodynamic lift by **turning the blade away from optimal angle**, preventing overspeed.

### **2. Efficiency Optimization**

Below rated wind speed, pitch control keeps the blade at an angle that produces **maximum lift-to-drag ratio** for maximum power output.

### **3. Turbine Safety**

During storms or extreme winds (>25 m/s), pitch system turns blades to **feathered** position (edge facing wind), almost eliminating aerodynamic force, and protecting blades, shaft, gearbox, and generator.

### **4. Load Management**

Pitch changes help reduce cyclic loads and mechanical fatigue, increasing turbine lifespan.

---

## **How It Works**

- Sensors (anemometer + wind vane) monitor wind.
    
- Controller sends command to pitch motors.
    
- Hydraulic/electric actuators rotate each blade independently.
    
- Blade angle continuously varies to maintain optimal aerodynamic performance.

# **5. Define Capacity Factor (Very Detailed)**

The **capacity factor** of a wind turbine measures how effectively it converts its **rated power** into actual usable power over time.

---

## **Definition**

Capacity factor is the ratio of the **actual electrical energy produced** by a wind turbine over a period to the **energy it would have produced if it operated at rated power continuously**.

[  
\text{Capacity Factor} = \frac{\text{Actual energy output}}{\text{Rated power × Total time}}  
]

---

## **What It Represents**

- Indicates **productivity** of turbine
    
- Accounts for wind variability, turbine downtime, maintenance, cut-in and cut-out events
    

---

## **Typical Values**

- Onshore wind farms: **25%–35%**
    
- Offshore wind farms: **40%–50%**
    
- Ideal (theoretical maximum): **100%** but never achieved
    

---

# **6. Define Tip-Speed Ratio and Solidity (Extremely Detailed)**

---

## **Tip-Speed Ratio (TSR)**

### **Definition**

TSR is the ratio of the **speed of a blade tip** to the **actual wind speed**.

[  
\lambda = \frac{\omega R}{V}  
]

Where:

- ( \omega ) = rotor angular speed (rad/s)
    
- ( R ) = rotor radius
    
- ( V ) = wind speed
    

### **Importance**

- Determines aerodynamic efficiency
    
- Optimum TSR is where turbine extracts **maximum power**
    
- High TSR → slender, fast-rotating blades (propeller type)
    
- Low TSR → drag-based, slower blades (Savonius)
    

### **Typical TSR Values**

- Propeller/HAWT: 6–10
    
- Darrieus: 4–6
    
- Savonius: 1–2
    

---

## **Solidity**

### **Definition**

Solidity represents the **ratio of total blade area** to the **total swept area** of rotor.

[  
\text{Solidity} = \frac{\text{Number of blades × Blade width}}{2\pi R}  
]

### **Interpretation**

- **High solidity**: Many wide blades
    
    - High starting torque
        
    - Low speed
        
    - Drag-based turbines (e.g., multiblade, Savonius)
        
- **Low solidity**: Few slender blades
    
    - High speed
        
    - High aerodynamic efficiency
        
    - Modern three-blade turbines
        

### **Relationship Between TSR & Solidity**

- High TSR → Low solidity
    
- Low TSR → High solidity
    

This relationship completely determines turbine behavior.

# **7. Explain Yaw Control and Define Nacelle and Wind Vane (Very Detailed)**

---

## **Yaw Control**

### **Definition**

Yaw control is the system that **rotates the entire wind turbine nacelle** so that the rotor is always facing into the wind.

### **Why It's Necessary**

- Maximum energy is captured only when the rotor is perpendicular to wind
    
- If misaligned, turbine extracts far less power
    
- Misalignment increases structural loads and decreases efficiency
    

### **Components of Yaw System**

1. **Yaw motor** (electric motor that turns nacelle)
    
2. **Yaw bearing** (massive ring that allows rotation)
    
3. **Yaw controller** (computer that decides when to rotate)
    
4. **Wind vane** (detects wind direction)
    

### **How It Works**

1. Wind vane detects wind direction.
    
2. Sends signal to controller.
    
3. Yaw motor rotates nacelle to face wind.
    
4. Nacelle locks into position until next adjustment.
    

---

## **Nacelle**

### **Definition**

The nacelle is the **housing on top of the tower** that contains all major mechanical and electrical components of the wind turbine.

### **Contains:**

- Gearbox
    
- Generator
    
- Low- and high-speed shafts
    
- Brake system
    
- Controller
    
- Yaw system components
    
- Cooling system
    
- Sensors
    

It is the “engine room” of the wind turbine.

---

## **Wind Vane**

### **Definition**

A wind vane is a device that **measures wind direction**.  
It usually has:

- A tail fin
    
- A pointer
    
- A pivoting shaft
    
- Electronic angle sensor
    

### **How It Works**

Wind pushes the tail → vane aligns with wind direction → sensor detects orientation → yaw controller adjusts nacelle.

# ⭐ **1. Derive the condition that results in the Betz Limit (VERY VERBOSE DERIVATION)**

The Betz Limit tells us the **maximum theoretical efficiency** with which any wind turbine can extract kinetic energy from moving air.  
This limit states that the **maximum fraction of power that can be extracted is 59.3%**, and this occurs when the downstream wind speed is **one-third of the upstream wind speed**.

This derivation depends entirely on the **law of conservation of mass and energy** applied to air moving through a rotor disk.

---

## **Step 1 — Define upstream and downstream wind speeds**

Let:

- ( V_1 ) = undisturbed (free-stream) wind speed before turbine
    
- ( V_2 ) = wind speed after leaving turbine
    
- ( V_m ) = wind speed through rotor plane (between (V_1) and (V_2))
    

By continuity and aerodynamics:  
[  
V_m = \frac{V_1 + V_2}{2}  
]

This is because air slows down gradually as it approaches the rotor, not suddenly.

---

## **Step 2 — Mass flow rate through the rotor**

Mass flow rate is:  
[  
\dot{m} = \rho A V_m = \rho A \frac{V_1 + V_2}{2}  
]

where

- ρ = air density
    
- A = swept area of turbine
    

---

## **Step 3 — Power extracted from wind is drop in kinetic energy**

Wind turbine extracts energy by reducing the velocity of air.

Kinetic energy change per second:

[  
P = \frac{1}{2} \dot{m}(V_1^2 - V_2^2)  
]

Substitute **(\dot{m})**:

[  
P = \frac{1}{2} \rho A \frac{V_1 + V_2}{2}(V_1^2 - V_2^2)  
]

Simplify:

[  
P = \frac{1}{4}\rho A(V_1 + V_2)(V_1^2 - V_2^2)  
]

Factor the bracket:

[  
V_1^2 - V_2^2 = (V_1 - V_2)(V_1 + V_2)  
]

So:

[  
P = \frac{1}{4} \rho A (V_1 - V_2)(V_1 + V_2)^2  
]

---

## **Step 4 — Define the power coefficient**

The total power in undisturbed wind:

[  
P_{wind} = \frac{1}{2} \rho A V_1^3  
]

Power coefficient:

[  
C_p = \frac{P}{P_{wind}}  
]

Substitute P:

[  
C_p = \frac{\frac{1}{4}\rho A (V_1 - V_2)(V_1 + V_2)^2}{\frac{1}{2}\rho A V_1^3}  
]

Cancel common terms:

[  
C_p = \frac{1}{2} (1 - a)(1+a)^2  
]

Let:

[  
a = \frac{V_2}{V_1}  
]

Then:

[  
C_p = \frac{1}{2}(1 - a)(1 + a)^2  
]

This is the classical **Betz equation**.

---

## **Step 5 — Maximize power coefficient w.r.t a**

Differentiate:

[  
\frac{dC_p}{da}=0  
]

After solving:

[  
a = \frac{1}{3}  
]

So:

[  
V_2 = \frac{1}{3}V_1  
]

---

## **Step 6 — Maximum CP value**

Substitute:

[  
C_{p,max} = \frac{16}{27}=0.593 = 59.3%  
]

---

# **Final Betz Result (Exam Statement)**

The **maximum theoretical efficiency** of a wind turbine occurs when the downstream wind speed falls to **one-third of the upstream wind speed**, giving a maximum power coefficient of:

[  
C_{p,max} = 0.593 = 59.3%  
]

This is the **Betz Limit**, proving that **no turbine can extract all the kinetic energy** in wind since the air must continue moving to allow more air to flow in.

# ⭐ **2. Classify WECS and Explain in Detail**

Wind Energy Conversion Systems (WECS) can be classified based on **axis orientation, purpose, speed control, generator configuration, grid interaction**, and **power control method**.

Below is a **deeply detailed classification**:

---

## **A. Based on Axis Orientation**

### **1. Horizontal Axis Wind Turbines (HAWT)**

- Axis parallel to wind direction
    
- Requires yaw system
    
- High efficiency (up to 45%)
    
- Modern wind farms use this type
    
- Large tower needed to reach strong winds
    
- Lift-based aerodynamics
    

### **2. Vertical Axis Wind Turbines (VAWT)**

- Axis perpendicular to wind direction
    
- Does NOT require yaw
    
- Works in turbulent, variable-direction wind
    
- Lower efficiency
    
- Generator at ground level (easy maintenance)
    

---

## **B. Based on Power Regulation**

### **1. Stall-Controlled**

- Blade shape inherently stalls at high wind speed
    
- Simple, no moving parts
    
- Low cost
    
- Lower efficiency
    

### **2. Pitch-Controlled**

- Blades rotate to reduce angle of attack
    
- High efficiency
    
- Controls power above rated wind speed
    
- Used in modern large turbines
    

---

## **C. Based on Operational Speed**

### **1. Constant-Speed WECS**

- Rotor speed fixed
    
- Easy to connect to grid
    
- Less energy capture
    

### **2. Variable-Speed WECS**

- Rotor speed adjusts with wind
    
- Uses power electronics
    
- More efficient technology
    
- Higher energy extraction
    

---

## **D. Based on Generator Type**

1. **Squirrel-cage induction generator**
    
2. **Doubly-fed induction generator (DFIG)**
    
3. **Synchronous generator**
    
4. **Permanent magnet synchronous generator (PMSG)**
    

Each has unique control and grid integration properties.

---

## **E. Based on Application**

1. **Standalone systems**
    

- Rural electrification
    
- Battery charging
    

2. **Grid-connected systems**
    

- Large wind farms
    
- Bulk power supply
    

3. **Hybrid systems**
    

- Wind + Solar + Diesel
    

---

This classification is complete and covers every KTU point.

# ⭐ **3. Construction & Working of Horizontal-Axis Wind Turbine (EXTREMELY VERBOSE)**

A HAWT consists of the following major components:

---

## **Construction (Describe in detail)**

### **1. Rotor Blades**

- Typically 2 or 3 blades
    
- Made from fiberglass composites
    
- Aerodynamic airfoil to generate lift
    
- Span can be 50–80 meters in large turbines
    
- Primary energy-capturing component
    

### **2. Hub**

- Connects blades to the low-speed shaft
    
- Rigid structure capable of handling blade loads
    

### **3. Nacelle**

Contains:

- Gearbox
    
- Generator
    
- Mechanical brake
    
- Electronic control system
    
- Anemometer and wind vane
    

### **4. Gearbox**

- Increases speed from ~30 rpm (rotor) to ~1500 rpm (generator)
    
- Planetary or parallel shaft type
    

### **5. Generator**

- Converts mechanical energy into electrical energy
    
- Usually induction or synchronous type
    

### **6. Yaw System**

- Rotates the nacelle to face wind
    
- Controlled by wind vane
    

### **7. Pitch Control System**

- Adjusts blade angle
    
- Maintains constant power at high winds
    
- Improves efficiency
    

### **8. Tower**

- Tall structure (80–120 m for utility turbines)
    
- Must resist strong dynamic wind loads
    
- Supports nacelle and rotor
    

### **9. Controller**

- Manages yaw, pitch, speed, grid synchronization
    

---

## **Working (Very Detailed)**

1. **Wind approaches the rotor**, creating a pressure difference on both sides of the blade — **lift force** is produced (similar to airplane wings).
    
2. Lift causes the blades to rotate the **hub**.
    
3. Hub turns the **low-speed shaft** (20–60 rpm).
    
4. Gearbox steps up this slow rotation to high speed (1000–1500 rpm).
    
5. High-speed shaft drives the **generator**, producing electricity.
    
6. Generated power passes through control systems → transformer → grid.
    
7. Yaw system continuously keeps the rotor facing wind.
    
8. Pitch system maintains efficiency and protects turbine in high wind speeds.
    
9. Brake system stops the turbine if wind exceeds cut-out speed.
    

This is the modern wind turbine design used worldwide.

# ⭐ **4. Construction and Working of Darrieus & Multiblade Turbines**

---

## **A. Darrieus Turbine (VAWT)**

### **Construction**

- Vertical central shaft
    
- Curved blades (egg-beater shape) or straight blades (H-shaped)
    
- Airfoil-shaped blades generate lift
    
- Bearings at top & bottom
    
- Generator usually at ground level
    
- No yaw needed
    
- Supported by guy wires for stability
    

### **Working**

- Wind flows across blades
    
- Blades experience **aerodynamic lift**, causing rotation
    
- Lift varies throughout the rotation → results in pulsating torque
    
- Typically requires external starting method
    
- Less sensitive to wind direction
    
- Can operate in turbulent areas (cities, valleys)
    

---

## **B. Multiblade Turbine**

### **Construction**

- Numerous blades arranged around hub (6–24 blades)
    
- Large surface area → high drag
    
- Mounted on tall lattice towers
    
- Simple mechanical design
    

### **Working**

- Drag-based mechanism
    
- Excellent **starting torque** — ideal for pumping water
    
- Low rotational speed (good for mechanical loads like pumps)
    
- Poor efficiency for electricity generation
    
- Historically used in farms for mechanical pumping

# ⭐ **5. Wind Speed Measurement – Anemometer and Wind Vane (Very Detailed)**

---

## **A. Cup Anemometer**

### **Construction**

- Three or four hemispherical cups
    
- Mounted on lightweight spokes around a central shaft
    
- Each cup catches wind and creates rotational torque
    
- A magnet or optical sensor detects rotations
    
- Electronics convert rpm → wind speed
    

### **Working**

- Wind strikes cups
    
- The difference in aerodynamic drag between advancing and retreating cups causes rotation
    
- Rotation rate ∝ wind speed
    
- Anemometer controller computes actual wind velocity
    
- Used to control yaw and pitch systems
    
- Also used for turbine power curve characterization
    

---

## **B. Wind Vane**

### **Construction**

- Arrow-like pointer with a large tail fin
    
- Mounted on a low-friction vertical shaft
    
- Contains potentiometer or encoder to detect angular position
    

### **Working**

- Wind pushes the tail
    
- Large surface area of tail aligns vane with wind direction
    
- Pointer indicates direction of incoming wind
    
- Turbine uses this information to activate **yaw motor** to face wind

- Drag-based

- High starting torque, low rpm

# ⭐ **9. Power–Speed Characteristics of a Wind Turbine (Very Verbose)**

A wind turbine’s power curve has **four operating regions**:

---

### **Region 1 — Below Cut-In Speed**

- Wind < 3–4 m/s
    
- Turbine does NOT generate power
    
- Only internal control electronics run
    
- Rotor may rotate slowly, but no power extraction
    
- Purpose: avoid inefficient low-speed operation
    

---

### **Region 2 — Between Cut-In and Rated Speed**

- Wind 4–12 m/s
    
- Power increases **approximately with cube of wind speed**
    
- Turbine maximizes aerodynamic efficiency
    
- Pitch system maintains optimal angle of attack
    
- This region determines **annual energy yield**
    

---

### **Region 3 — Rated Region**

- Wind 12–25 m/s
    
- Turbine reaches **maximum rated power**
    
- Pitch system actively **sheds excess energy**
    
- Ensures generator and blades are not overloaded
    
- Power output is constant, independent of wind speed
    

---

### **Region 4 — Above Cut-Out Speed**

- Wind > 25 m/s
    
- Turbine shuts down automatically
    
- Blades feather
    
- Brakes may be applied
    
- Safety protection from extreme winds
    
- Prevents structural damage
    

---

Overall, the power curve demonstrates:

1. No power at low speeds
    
2. Fast cubic power rise
    
3. Flat plateau at rated power
    
4. Protective shutdown at high speed
