# **1. Environmental Impacts of OTEC Plants**

Ocean Thermal Energy Conversion (OTEC) plants use the **temperature difference** between warm surface water and cold deep seawater to generate electricity.  
Although OTEC is “clean energy,” its operation does create several **localized environmental impacts** that must be carefully studied.

## **A. Impacts Due to Large Seawater Flow Rates**

OTEC plants pump **huge quantities of seawater**:

- Warm surface water (~26°C)
    
- Cold deep water (~4–10°C)
    

The pumping and discharge may cause:

### **1. Local Thermal Pollution**

When cold deep water is discharged near the surface:

- It reduces surface temperature locally
    
- Can affect phytoplankton concentration
    
- May disturb heat-dependent marine organisms near the surface
    

Similarly, mixing warm water into deeper layers can disturb deep-water ecosystems that rely on cold temperatures.

### **2. Altered Marine Nutrient Levels**

Deep ocean water is **nutrient rich** (nitrates, phosphates).  
Bringing this up to the surface may:

- Increase algal growth (possible eutrophication)
    
- Change the species composition of plankton
    
- Boost productivity temporarily but disrupt long-term ecological balance
    

### **3. Marine Life Entrainment**

Pumping large volumes of water can **suck in small marine organisms**:

- Plankton
    
- Larvae
    
- Small fish  
    This can influence food chains.
    

---

## **B. Chemical Impacts**

### **4. Release of Working Fluids (Closed Cycle)**

Closed-cycle OTEC uses ammonia.  
Leakage poses:

- Toxicity risks to marine organisms
    
- Contamination of surrounding water
    
- Safety hazards to maintenance personnel
    

### **5. Corrosion and Biofouling Chemicals**

Anti-fouling agents used on heat exchangers and pipelines can enter the marine environment.

---

## **C. Physical & Acoustic Impacts**

### **6. Noise Pollution**

Large pumps and turbines create underwater noise that may disturb:

- Marine mammals
    
- Fish that navigate by sound
    

### **7. Changes in Local Currents**

Pumping and discharge create **artificial water currents**, possibly affecting:

- Sediment transport
    
- Larval dispersal
    
- Local fish migration patterns
    

---

## **D. Positive Environmental Effects**

### **8. Production of Freshwater**

Open-cycle OTEC can produce **desalinated freshwater**, helping water-scarce areas.

### **9. Reduced Greenhouse Gas Emissions**

OTEC avoids fossil fuels entirely.

# **2. Distinguish Between Open-Cycle and Closed-Cycle OTEC (Very Detailed)**

|Feature|**Open-Cycle OTEC**|**Closed-Cycle OTEC**|
|---|---|---|
|**Working Principle**|Uses **seawater as the working fluid**. Warm seawater is flash-evaporated under vacuum.|Uses a **separate working fluid** (ammonia or refrigerant) with low boiling point.|
|**Working Fluid**|Warm seawater → steam → turbine → condensed by cold seawater|Ammonia or refrigerant circulates in a closed loop|
|**Turbine Input**|Low-pressure steam from warm seawater|High-pressure vapor from evaporated ammonia|
|**Condenser Input**|Cold deep seawater|Same cold seawater through heat exchanger|
|**Output Benefits**|Produces **electricity + desalinated freshwater**|Produces only electricity|
|**Plant Size**|Turbine must handle large volumes of low-density steam → turbine is large|Turbine smaller because ammonia vapor density is high|
|**Efficiency**|Slightly lower due to low steam temperature|Slightly higher due to efficient working fluid|
|**Environmental Concerns**|Thermal discharge, entrainment, mixing nutrients|Ammonia leakage hazard|
|**Complexity**|Mechanically simple but large equipment|More complex heat exchangers|

# **3. Components of a Tidal Power Plant (Highly Detailed)**

A tidal power plant converts the **rise and fall of tides** into electricity. Main components are:

## **1. Basin (Tidal Reservoir)**

A large enclosed area that stores seawater when tide rises and releases it when tide falls.

## **2. Barrage (Dam-like Structure)**

Constructed across an estuary or bay to create a basin.  
Supports turbines, sluice gates, and control buildings.

## **3. Sluice Gates**

Large gates that control water flow into or out of the basin:

- Open during filling/emptying
    
- Closed during power generation
    

## **4. Turbines**

Placed in the barrage.  
Operate when water flows through them either during:

- **Inflow (flood tide)**
    
- **Outflow (ebb tide)**  
    Depending on scheme (ebb, flood, or two-way generation).
    

## **5. Power House**

Contains:

- Turbine-generator sets
    
- Control systems
    
- Transformers
    

## **6. Generator**

Coupled to turbine, converts rotational mechanical power to electricity.

## **7. Transformers and Grid Connection**

Steps up voltage and integrates with electrical grid.

## **8. Fish Passages**

Allow marine organisms to move without being harmed.

# **4. Principle of Tidal Power Generation (Deep Explanation)**

Tidal power generation is based on the **gravitational interaction between the Earth–Moon–Sun system**, which causes **periodic rising and falling of sea levels**.

The principle can be stated as:

> **Tidal energy is generated by capturing the potential energy created by the difference in water levels between high tide and low tide.**

During high tide, water is allowed to enter a basin.  
During low tide, water is released from the basin through turbines.

### **Energy Conversion Steps**

1. **High tide:**
    
    - Sea level rises
        
    - Water flows into basin through sluice gates
        
2. **Gates close:**
    
    - Basin traps high-level water
        
3. **Low tide occurs:**
    
    - Outside sea level drops
        
    - Basin water is at higher level than sea
        
4. **Release through turbine:**
    
    - Water flows out due to head difference
        
    - Turbine rotates → generator produces electricity
        

This head difference (usually 3–10 m) is the **potential energy source**.

# **5. Criteria for Selecting OTEC Sites (Very Detailed)**

OTEC requires a strong **temperature gradient** between surface and deep water.

## **A. Temperature Difference**

### **1. Minimum ΔT Requirement**

- Surface water: ~25–28°C
    
- Deep water (1000 m depth): ~4–7°C
    
- Minimum required **temperature difference = 20°C**  
    This is the most critical factor.
    

---

## **B. Oceanographic Conditions**

### **2. Deep Water Close to Shore**

Site must have **1000 m depth close to land** to reduce cost of long cold-water pipes.

### **3. Available warm surface water**

Areas in **tropical oceans between 20°N and 20°S** are ideal.

### **4. Stable Temperature Profile**

Temperature gradient must be stable year-round, not seasonal.

---

## **C. Environmental Conditions**

### **5. Low Wave Intensity**

Calm seas reduce mechanical stress on pipes and floating platforms.

### **6. Minimal Cyclone Risk**

Storms can damage platforms and pipelines.

---

## **D. Economic and Infrastructure Considerations**

### **7. Proximity to Power Demand**

Near islands, coastal cities, or resorts.

### **8. Availability of marine facilities**

Harbors, maintenance stations, and marine engineering support.

# **1. Closed-Cycle OTEC – Explanation, Diagram (verbal), Merits & Limitations**

Closed-cycle OTEC uses a **working fluid with a low boiling point**, such as **ammonia**, instead of seawater.  
The temperature difference between **warm surface water (~27°C)** and **cold deep seawater (~4–7°C)** drives evaporation and condensation of the working fluid.

---

## **A. Working Principle (Detailed)**

1. **Warm surface seawater** is pumped through an **evaporator heat exchanger**.
    
2. The working fluid (ammonia) **boils at low temperature**, forming high-pressure vapor.
    
3. This high-pressure vapor drives a **turbine**, which is connected to a **generator**.
    
4. After expansion, the low-pressure vapor enters a **condenser**.
    
5. **Cold deep seawater** is pumped through the condenser, cooling and **condensing the vapor back into liquid**.
    
6. Liquid ammonia is **pumped back to the evaporator**, and the cycle repeats.
    

---

- Left box: “Warm Surface Water Heat Exchanger (Evaporator)”
    
- Connected to “Vapor Turbine” → “Generator”
    
- Exhaust vapor goes to “Cold Water Condenser”
    
- Liquid returns to “Pump” → evaporator
    
- Warm water intake at top, cold water intake at bottom with long pipe drawn to 1000 m depth.
    

---

## **C. Merits**

- Can operate **24 hours** continuously due to stable ocean temperature gradient.
    
- Turbine size is **small** because ammonia vapor is dense.
    
- No need for vacuum chambers.
    
- More efficient than open cycle.
    
- Modular and can be installed on land or floating platforms.
    

---

## **D. Limitations**

- **Ammonia leakage** is hazardous and harmful to marine life.
    
- Requires **very large heat exchangers** (temperature difference is small).
    
- Expensive cold-water pipe (1000 m long).
    
- Low thermodynamic efficiency (2–4%).
    
- High capital cost and marine engineering challenges.

# **2. Open-Cycle OTEC – Explanation & Diagram**

Open-cycle OTEC uses **seawater itself as the working fluid**.  
Warm seawater is evaporated at very low pressure to produce steam.

---

## **A. Working Principle**

1. Warm surface seawater is pumped into a **vacuum evaporator chamber**.
    
2. Under low pressure, warm water **flash-evaporates** into low-pressure steam.
    
3. This steam expands and drives a **low-pressure turbine**.
    
4. After the turbine, the steam enters a **condenser** that uses cold deep seawater.
    
5. Steam condenses into **desalinated fresh water**.
    
6. Saltwater brine is discharged back to the ocean.
    

---

## **B. Diagram Description (sketchable)**

- Steam line → turbine → generator.
    
- Turbine exhaust → cold seawater condenser.
    
- Freshwater outlet drawn.
    
- Deep cold-water pipe shown reaching 1000 m depth.
    

---

## **C. Merits**

- Produces **freshwater + electricity**.
    
- Working fluid (seawater) is non-toxic.
    
- No ammonia or chemicals involved.
    

---

## **D. Limitations**

- Turbine must handle **huge volume of low-density steam**, making it large.
    
- Efficiency very low.
    
- Maintaining large vacuum chamber is difficult.
    
- Scaling and biofouling problems.

# **3. Hybrid OTEC System (How Open + Closed Combine)**

Hybrid OTEC combines the benefits of **open-cycle** and **closed-cycle** systems.

---

## **A. Working Principle (Highly Detailed)**

### **Stage 1 – Open Cycle Stage**

- Warm seawater flash-evaporates in a vacuum chamber.
    
- Low-pressure steam drives a **steam turbine** or is used directly as a heat source.
    

### **Stage 2 – Closed Cycle Stage**

- The steam from open-cycle evaporator is used to **heat the low-boiling working fluid** (ammonia) in a closed-cycle evaporator.
    

Thus:

- Open-cycle steam acts as the _heat source_ for closed-cycle ammonia evaporation.
    
- Ammonia vapor drives a **high-efficiency turbine**.
    

### **How They Combine**

- Open cycle provides the heat to evaporate the ammonia.
    
- Closed cycle uses evaporated ammonia for efficient power generation.
    
- The steam from open cycle eventually condenses in cold water, producing freshwater.
    

Thus, hybrid OTEC yields:

1. **Electricity from open cycle steam turbine**
    
2. **Higher-efficiency electricity from closed cycle ammonia turbine**
    
3. **Desalinated freshwater**
    

---

## **B. Diagram Description**

- Left: open-cycle flash evaporator → steam line to heat exchanger.
    
- Middle: heat exchanger evaporates ammonia → ammonia turbine → condenser → pump.
    
- Right: cold-water condenser → freshwater outlet.

# **4. Classify Tidal Power Plants & Explain Double-Basin Operation (with diagram)**

---

## **A. Classification of Tidal Power Plants**

1. **Single-basin, single-effect**
    
    - Generation occurs only during ebb tide (water flows out).
        
2. **Single-basin, double-effect**
    
    - Generation occurs during both inflow and outflow.
        
3. **Double-basin schemes**
    
    - Two basins: high basin and low basin.
        
    - Power is generated almost continuously.
        
4. **Tidal stream devices**
    
    - Underwater turbines placed in tidal currents.
        

---

# **Double-Basin Tidal Power Plant – Explanation (Very Detailed)**

A double-basin plant uses **two basins at different levels** so that electricity can be generated almost continuously.

---

## **Construction**

- **Basin A** (Upper basin)
    
- **Basin B** (Lower basin)
    
- **Barrage** separating basins
    
- **Turbine House** between basins
    
- **Sluice gates** for both basins
    
- Sea barrages connecting each basin to the ocean
    

---

## **Working**

### **Step 1: High Tide**

- Sea level rises.
    
- Upper basin is filled through sluice gates.
    
- Lower basin remains isolated.
    

### **Step 2: Generation Phase 1**

- Water flows from upper basin to lower basin through turbines → generates power.
    

### **Step 3: Low Tide**

- Lower basin discharges water into sea (through sluices).
    
- Upper basin isolated.
    

### **Step 4: Generation Phase 2**

- As tide rises again, water flows from sea to lower basin (if double effect).
    
- Then from upper basin to lower basin → power generation continues.
    

---

- Upper basin at left, lower basin at right.

# **5. Limitations of Tidal Power Generation (Very Detailed)**

- **High capital cost** for barrages and civil structures.
    
- **Suitable sites are rare** (high tidal range needed: 5–10 m).
    
- **Tides are intermittent** (twice daily), causing non-uniform power generation unless double-basin is used.
    
- **Long construction time** (5–10 years).
    
- **Environmental impacts** on estuaries and marine habitats.
    
- **Sedimentation & siltation** reduce basin capacity.
    
- **Turbine damage** from marine corrosion & debris.
    
- **Navigation interference** for ships.
    
- **High maintenance** in saltwater conditions.

# **6. Tidal Power Generation Modes & Their Limitations**

Three main modes:

---

## **A. Ebb-Generation Mode**

### **Working**

- Basin filled at high tide.
    
- Sluices closed.
    
- At low tide, water flows out through turbines → generation.
    

### **Limitations**

- Power only during falling tide.
    
- Long idle periods.
    
- Non-continuous supply.
    

---

## **B. Flood-Generation Mode**

### **Working**

- During rising tide, water flows **from sea to basin** through turbines.
    
- Generation occurs only during incoming tide.
    

### **Limitations**

- Less commonly used.
    
- Lower efficiency.
    
- Power only during flood tide.
    

---

## **C. Double-Effect Mode**

### **Working**

- Turbines designed to run in **both directions**.
    
- Generates during both flood and ebb.
    

### **Limitations**

- Requires expensive reversible turbines.
    
- Complex control.
    
- More maintenance.
    

---

### **Limitations**

- Very high civil cost.
