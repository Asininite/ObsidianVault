# **1. Define biomass gasification.**

**Biomass gasification** is a **thermochemical conversion process** in which solid biomass (wood, crop residues, coconut shells, sawdust, municipal waste, etc.) is **partially oxidized** at high temperatures—typically **800–1000°C**—in a controlled environment with **limited oxygen or air**.

In gasification, the biomass **does not fully burn**.  
Instead, the limited supply of oxygen ensures **incomplete combustion**, producing a fuel-rich combustible gas mixture known as **producer gas**.

Gasification converts solid biomass into a **cleaner, more versatile gaseous fuel** that can be used for:

- Power generation in IC engines
    
- Heat applications (kilns, dryers, furnaces)
    
- Electricity generation in small gasifier-engine systems
    
- Syngas for industrial operations
    

In summary:  
**Biomass gasification = conversion of solid biomass into a combustible gas using partial oxidation at high temperature.**

---

# **2. What is producer gas and how is it produced?**

### **Producer Gas – Definition**

Producer gas is a **low-calorific-value combustible gas** generated from the gasification of biomass or coal under conditions of **partial air supply**.

### **Composition (typical):**

- **CO (Carbon monoxide):** 15–20%
    
- **H₂ (Hydrogen):** 10–15%
    
- **CH₄ (Methane):** 1–5%
    
- **CO₂ (Carbon dioxide):** 10–15%
    
- **N₂ (Nitrogen):** 45–55% (from air)
    

Because air is used as the oxidizing medium, nitrogen dilutes the gas, lowering its calorific value (≈ 4–6 MJ/m³).

### **How it is produced**

Producer gas is formed inside a gasifier during **partial combustion** of biomass. The steps are:

1. **Biomass is fed into the gasifier.**
    
2. A **restricted supply of air/oxygen** is admitted.
    
3. Biomass undergoes drying, pyrolysis, oxidation, and reduction.
    
4. During reduction, CO, H₂, CH₄ are formed.
    
5. These gases mix to become **producer gas**, which exits through an outlet and is cooled and filtered.
    

Thus:  
**Producer gas = gas mixture formed when biomass undergoes partial combustion at high temperature in a gasifier.**

# **3. List major sources of biogas.**

Biogas is produced through the **anaerobic digestion** of organic materials. Major sources include:

### **Animal Wastes**

- Cow dung (most common)
    
- Poultry litter
    
- Pig manure
    
- Goat and sheep manure
    

### **Agricultural Residues**

- Vegetable wastes
    
- Fruit processing residues
    
- Sugarcane press mud
    

### **Kitchen and Food Wastes**

- Household food scraps
    
- Hotel and restaurant waste
    
- Canteen waste
    

### **Municipal Solid Wastes**

- Organic fraction of city waste
    
- Market vegetable waste
    

### **Industrial Effluents**

- Distillery wastewater
    
- Dairy industry wastewater
    
- Slaughterhouse waste
    
- Paper and pulp industry sludge
    

### **Sewage**

- Sewage sludge from wastewater treatment plants
    

All the above materials contain organic matter that can be decomposed by anaerobic bacteria to produce biogas.

# **4. Define pyrolysis.**

**Pyrolysis** is the **thermal decomposition of biomass in the complete absence (or near-absence) of oxygen**, typically at temperatures between **400°C and 800°C**.

During pyrolysis:

- Biomass breaks down into  
    • **Charcoal (solid)**  
    • **Pyrolysis oil / bio-oil (liquid)**  
    • **Syngas (gas mixture)**
    

No combustion occurs because oxygen is not present.  
Thermal energy causes **chemical bonds in biomass** (cellulose, hemicellulose, lignin) to break, forming volatile vapors and char.

Pyrolysis is important because:

- It produces **bio-oil** (can be used as fuel or chemical feedstock)
    
- Produces **char** (used as solid fuel or soil amendment)
    
- Produces **fuel gas** suitable for heating
    

Thus, pyrolysis is a key biomass conversion step occurring **before gasification**.

# **5. Mention the stages involved in gasification.**

Biomass gasification occurs in **four main thermal zones**, arranged vertically inside the gasifier.  
These stages take place as the biomass moves downward and temperature increases.

### **Stage 1 – Drying Zone (100–200°C)**

- Moisture is removed from biomass.
    
- No chemical reaction occurs.
    
- Only evaporation of water.
    

### **Stage 2 – Pyrolysis Zone (200–500°C)**

- Biomass decomposes into char, tar vapors, and volatiles.
    
- Produces gases like CO, CO₂, CH₄ in small amounts.
    

### **Stage 3 – Oxidation/Combustion Zone (900–1100°C)**

- Limited air causes partial combustion.
    
- Reactions:  
    • C + O₂ → CO₂  
    • CO + ½O₂ → CO₂
    
- Generates intense heat required for later reduction.
    

### **Stage 4 – Reduction Zone (700–900°C)**

- Hot CO₂ and H₂O react with char to form CO and H₂.  
    • C + CO₂ → 2CO  
    • C + H₂O → CO + H₂  
    • CO + H₂O ↔ CO₂ + H₂
    
- These reactions form **producer gas**.
    

Summarized:  
**Drying → Pyrolysis → Oxidation → Reduction**.

# **6. Describe factors influencing biogas production.**

Biogas production depends heavily on biological, chemical, and environmental conditions inside the digester.

### **(1) Temperature**

- Anaerobic bacteria work best at:  
    • Mesophilic range: **30–40°C**  
    • Thermophilic range: **50–60°C**
    
- Too low → slow gas production
    
- Too high → bacteria die
    

### **(2) pH Value**

- Optimum pH: **6.5 – 7.5**
    
- Acidic or alkaline conditions inhibit microbes.
    

### **(3) Carbon–Nitrogen Ratio (C:N ratio)**

- Ideal C:N = **25:1 to 30:1**
    
- Too much nitrogen → ammonia toxicity
    
- Too much carbon → slow digestion
    

### **(4) Type of Feedstock**

- Easily degradable organic wastes produce more biogas.
    
- Lignin-rich material (wood) produces little gas.
    

### **(5) Retention Time (HRT)**

- Typical: 30–50 days
    
- Short HRT → incomplete digestion
    
- Long HRT → stable and higher gas yield
    

### **(6) Mixing and Stirring**

- Prevents scum formation
    
- Uniform microbial activity
    
- Prevents settling of solids
    

### **(7) Toxic Substances**

- Detergents, antibiotics, salts, pesticides inhibit bacterial growth.
    

### **(8) Moisture Content**

- Ideal moisture ≈ 80–90%
    
- Too dry → bacteria inactive
    
- Too wet → dilution reduces temperature
    

### **(9) Loading Rate**

- Overloading causes acid formation → reduces pH → reduces gas.
    

Together, these factors determine the **quantity, rate, and quality** of biogas generation.

# **1. Biomass Conversion Technologies**

Biomass conversion refers to the processes through which raw biomass is transformed into useful forms of energy such as heat, electricity, biogas, or liquid fuels. The following are the major conversion technologies:

---

## **A. Thermochemical Conversion**

Uses **heat** and **chemical reactions** to convert biomass.

### **(1) Combustion**

- Biomass burned in excess air.
    
- Produces heat → steam → electricity.
    
- Used in boilers, furnaces, domestic stoves.
    

### **(2) Gasification**

- Biomass converted into producer gas (CO, H₂, CH₄) at **800–1000°C** with **limited air**.
    
- Gas can run engines, gas turbines, or be used for heating.
    

### **(3) Pyrolysis**

- Heating biomass **without oxygen** (400–800°C).
    
- Produces **bio-oil**, **charcoal**, and **pyrolysis gas**.
    

### **(4) Liquefaction**

- High-pressure conversion into **bio-oil** or liquid hydrocarbons.
    
- Occurs in presence of solvents.
    

---

## **B. Biochemical Conversion**

Uses **microbial action** rather than heat.

### **(1) Anaerobic Digestion**

- Organic material decomposed by bacteria → **biogas** (CH₄ + CO₂).
    
- Uses cow dung, kitchen waste, sewage.
    

### **(2) Fermentation**

- Sugars/starches converted to **ethanol** using yeast.
    
- Used for bioethanol fuel.
    

---

## **C. Chemical Conversion**

### **Transesterification**

- Oils/fats converted into **biodiesel** using alcohol + catalyst.
    
- Produces biodiesel + glycerol.
    

---

**Summary:**  
Combustion, gasification, pyrolysis, liquefaction, anaerobic digestion, fermentation, and transesterification are the major biomass conversion routes.

# **2. Updraft Gasifier – Construction & Working**

An updraft gasifier is the **simplest and oldest** type of gasifier.

---

## **Construction**

- **Cylindrical reactor** with top feed and bottom air inlet.
    
- **Grate** at bottom supports biomass.
    
- **Ash pit** below grate to collect ash.
    
- **Air inlet** at bottom (through tuyeres).
    
- **Gas outlet** at the top.
    
- **Fuel hopper** at the top for continuous feeding.
    

---

## **Working**

As air enters from the bottom and biomass from the top, **four thermal zones** are formed:

### **(1) Drying Zone (Top)**

- Hot gases rising from below dry the incoming biomass.
    

### **(2) Pyrolysis Zone**

- Biomass decomposes to char, tars, volatiles.
    

### **(3) Reduction Zone**

- CO₂ and H₂O react with hot char → CO, H₂, CH₄.
    

### **(4) Oxidation Zone (Bottom)**

- Air reacts with char → generates heat.
    

### **Gas Flow:**

Air moves **upwards**; gas also flows **upwards** → exits from the top.

---

## **Advantages**

- Simple construction
    
- High efficiency
    
- Can handle high-moisture fuels
    

## **Disadvantages**

- Gas has **high tar content** → unsuitable for engines without cleaning.
    

---

# **3. Downdraft Gasifier – Construction & Working**

Downdraft gasifiers are widely used for **engine-grade gas** because of **low tar** output.

---

## **Construction**

- Vertical reactor.
    
- Biomass fed from top.
    
- **Throat** (narrow region) in middle.
    
- Air inlets located around throat.
    
- Gas outlet at bottom.
    
- Grate and ash pit at bottom.
    

---

## **Working**

Biomass moves downward, and air enters at the throat.

Zones form in this sequence:

### **(1) Drying Zone (top)**

Incoming fuel dries by convection from hot gases.

### **(2) Pyrolysis Zone**

Fuel decomposes → volatiles + char.

### **(3) Oxidation Zone (near throat)**

Air reacts with char → produces heat.

### **(4) Reduction Zone (below throat)**

Hot gases pass through hot char bed → tars crack → CO, H₂, CH₄ form.

### **Gas Path:**

Gas flows **downwards**, along with biomass → exits from bottom.

---

## **Advantages**

- **Low tar content** (best for IC engines)
    
- High-quality producer gas
    
- Stable gas output
    

## **Disadvantages**

- Cannot handle very wet fuel
    
- Sensitive to fuel size and uniformity
    

---

# **4. Fluidized-Bed Gasifier – Construction & Operation**

Fluidized-bed gasifiers are used for **large-scale**, **uniform-temperature**, **high-efficiency** gasification.

---

## **Construction**

- **Reactor vessel** filled with inert bed material (sand, ash).
    
- **Air distributor plate** at bottom.
    
- Fuel feeding system.
    
- Cyclone separator for char carryover.
    
- Gas outlet.
    
- Ash extraction system.
    

---

## **Operation**

1. Air/steam is blown from the bottom.
    
2. Bed material becomes “fluidized” (behaves like boiling liquid).
    
3. Biomass is fed into the hot bed.
    
4. Turbulent mixing ensures:
    
    - Uniform temperature
        
    - Complete gasification
        
    - Enhanced reaction rates
        

Producer gas exits at the top after passing through cyclone separators.

---

## **Advantages**

- Handles wide variety of fuels
    
- High efficiency
    
- No hotspots (uniform temperature)
    
- Large-scale applications
    

## **Disadvantages**

- Complex design
    
- High dust loading in gas

# **5. Fixed-Dome and Floating-Drum Biogas Plants – Construction & Working**

These are the most common rural biogas plant designs.

---

# **(A) Fixed-Dome Biogas Plant (Janata Type)**

## **Construction**

- Underground digester tank (brick/masonry).
    
- Dome-shaped gas holder made of concrete (non-movable).
    
- Inlet pipe for feeding slurry.
    
- Outlet displacement chamber.
    
- Gas outlet pipe at top of dome.
    

---

## **Working**

- Biomass slurry fed through inlet.
    
- Anaerobic digestion produces gas.
    
- Gas collects in the dome → pressure builds.
    
- Slurry gets displaced into the outlet tank.
    
- Gas pressure varies depending on gas volume.
    

---

# **(B) Floating-Drum Biogas Plant (KVIC Type)**

## **Construction**

- Digester tank (brick masonry).
    
- **Steel floating drum** as gas holder → moves up/down.
    
- Guide frame to hold drum vertical.
    
- Inlet and outlet tanks.
    
- Gas outlet at top of the drum.
    

---

## **Working**

- Gas produced pushes the drum upward.
    
- Drum weight provides constant pressure.
    
- Gas withdrawn at constant pressure.
    
- Slurry flows in and out based on gas volume.

# **7. Stages of Biogas Production (Anaerobic Digestion)**

Biogas formation occurs in **four biological stages**:

### **(1) Hydrolysis**

Complex organic matter → simple sugars, amino acids, fatty acids.  
Large molecules are broken down by enzymes.

### **(2) Acidogenesis**

Simple compounds → volatile fatty acids (acetic, propionic), alcohols, CO₂, H₂, NH₃.  
Performed by acid-forming bacteria.

### **(3) Acetogenesis**

Volatile fatty acids → acetic acid + H₂ + CO₂.  
Prepares feed for methanogens.

### **(4) Methanogenesis**

Methanogenic bacteria convert:

- Acetic acid → CH₄ + CO₂
    
- H₂ + CO₂ → CH₄  
    This step produces the majority of **biogas (60% CH₄, 40% CO₂)**.

# **8. Energy Generation from Municipal Solid Waste (MSW)**

MSW contains biodegradable and combustible matter that can be converted into energy.

### **Steps:**

### **(1) Collection and Segregation**

Organic waste separated from metals, plastics, glass.

### **(2) Processing**

Shredding, drying, size reduction.

### **(3) Conversion Methods**

- **Incineration:** Direct combustion → high-pressure steam → electricity.
    
- **Refuse-Derived Fuel (RDF):** Waste compressed into pellets → burned in boilers.
    
- **Anaerobic digestion:** Organic portion → biogas → engines/turbines.
    
- **Gasification/Pyrolysis:** Converts MSW to syngas → electricity.
    

### **(4) Power Generation**

Steam turbine or gas engine converts thermal or chemical energy to electricity.

# **9. Biomass to Ethanol – Process Explanation**

Bioethanol is produced by fermenting biomass high in **sugars, starch, or cellulose**.

### **Steps:**

### **(1) Pretreatment**

- Size reduction
    
- Heat/chemical treatment
    
- Breaks down lignocellulosic structure
    

### **(2) Hydrolysis**

Converts complex carbohydrates into simple sugars:

- Starch → glucose
    
- Cellulose → glucose
    
- Hemicellulose → xylose
    

Enzymatic hydrolysis commonly used.

### **(3) Fermentation**

Yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae) converts sugars → ethanol + CO₂.

[  
C_6H_{12}O_6 → 2C_2H_5OH + 2CO_2  
]

### **(4) Distillation**

Ethanol separated from water to achieve ~95% purity.

### **(5) Dehydration**

Further purification using molecular sieves to reach >99% fuel-grade ethanol.

### **(6) Storage and Distribution**

Ethanol blended with petrol (E10, E20) or used as standalone biofuel.
