## 🌊 OTEC (Ocean Thermal Energy Conversion)

### **Introduction**

- OTEC is a process that generates electricity using the **temperature difference** between warm surface seawater (25–30°C) and cold deep seawater (5–8°C at ~1000 m depth).
    
- Oceans cover ~70% of Earth and absorb ~80% of solar energy, making them a vast energy reservoir.
    
- A temperature difference of at least **20°C** is required for feasible operation.
    
- Unlike solar/wind, OTEC provides **continuous 24/7 power**, especially in tropical and subtropical regions (20°N–20°S latitude).
    

---

### **Principle**

- Works like a conventional **Rankine cycle power plant**, but with **seawater as heat source and sink**:
    
    - Warm surface seawater → evaporates working fluid or itself (in open cycle).
        
    - Expanding vapor drives a **low-pressure turbine** → generates electricity.
        
    - Cold deep seawater → condenses vapor back into liquid → cycle repeats.
        

---

### **Types of OTEC Systems**

1. **Open Cycle (Claude Cycle)**
    
    - Warm seawater is **flash-evaporated** in a vacuum chamber → produces steam.
        
    - Steam drives a turbine.
        
    - Cold deep water condenses steam.
        
    - By-product: **desalinated fresh water**.
        
    - Disadvantage: turbines must handle **low-pressure steam**.
        
2. **Closed Cycle (Anderson Cycle)**
    
    - Uses a **working fluid** (like ammonia) with a low boiling point.
        
    - Warm seawater heats fluid → vapor drives turbine.
        
    - Cold seawater condenses vapor → fluid is reused.
        
    - Advantage: compact design, no corrosion from seawater on turbine.
        
3. **Hybrid Cycle**
    
    - Combines **open + closed cycle**.
        
    - Warm seawater flash-evaporates → steam vaporizes a secondary working fluid (like ammonia).
        
    - Produces **both electricity + desalinated water**.
        

---

### **Site Selection Criteria**

- Temperature difference ≥ **20°C**.
    
- Ocean depth of 600–1000 m **close to shore** (to minimize pipe length).
    
- Stable seabed and low seismic activity.
    
- Availability of **grid connection** or local demand.
    
- Preferably tropical islands/coastal regions (Hawaii, India, Philippines).
    

---

### **Problems: Biofouling**

- Seawater contains **microorganisms, slime, and sediments**.
    
- These deposit on heat exchangers → increase **thermal resistance** → reduce efficiency.
    
- Solutions: **chlorination, mechanical cleaning, optimized flow design**.
    
- Biofouling is worse in **closed cycle** plants since water flow is lower.
    

---

### **Advantages**

- Continuous, reliable power (not weather-dependent).
    
- Clean energy → CO₂ emissions <1% of fossil fuel plants.
    
- By-products:
    
    - **Desalinated water** (1 MW OTEC → 4500 m³/day).
        
    - **Deep-sea cold water** for **air-conditioning**.
        
    - **Nutrient-rich cold water** for **mariculture (fish farming)**.
        
- Potential: ~577,000 MW globally.
    

---

### **Limitations**

- **Low efficiency** (~2–3%) since ΔT is small.
    
- **High installation cost** (offshore pipes, platforms).
    
- Large structures must withstand **storms & corrosion**.
    
- Limited to **tropical regions**.
    
- Still at **demonstration stage** (not full-scale commercial).
    

---

### **Applications**

- **Electricity generation** (primary).
    
- **Fresh water production** (open/hybrid cycles).
    
- **Mariculture** (cold nutrient-rich water for fish/algae).
    
- **Air conditioning & refrigeration** (deep cold water).
    
- **Mineral extraction** (ammonia, hydrogen, methanol).

# ⭐ **HYBRID CYCLE OTEC (Ocean Thermal Energy Conversion)**

A **Hybrid Cycle OTEC** combines the **advantages of both Open Cycle and Closed Cycle OTEC**.

It is designed to:

- Produce **both electricity** _and_
    
- Generate **freshwater** (desalinated water)
    

This is the key idea behind the hybrid system.

---

# ⭐ **HOW DOES IT COMBINE OPEN & CLOSED CYCLES?**

A hybrid cycle has **two stages**:

---

# 🔹 **Stage 1: Open Cycle Flash Evaporation**

- Warm surface seawater (≈ 25°C) enters a **low-pressure evaporator**.
    
- Due to the vacuum, it **flashes into steam**.
    
- This steam is **nearly pure water vapor** (desalinated).
    
- The low-pressure steam is used to **heat a working fluid** in a secondary heat exchanger.
    

👉 This part is **same as Open Cycle OTEC**, except here the steam does **not** directly run a turbine.

---

# 🔹 **Stage 2: Closed Cycle Power Generation**

- The steam generated in Stage 1 passes through an **evaporator** where it transfers heat to a **working fluid** (like ammonia).
    
- The working fluid **vaporizes**.
    
- The vapor drives a **turbine**, producing **electricity**.
    
- After expansion, it is **condensed** using deep cold seawater.
    

👉 This part is **same as Closed Cycle OTEC**, where a working fluid drives the turbine.

---

# ⭐ **SIMPLIFIED FLOW OF HYBRID CYCLE**

1. Warm seawater → flash evaporator → low-pressure steam
    
2. Steam → heats ammonia (or working fluid) → ammonia vapor
    
3. Ammonia vapor → turbine → generator → electricity
    
4. Ammonia vapor → condenser → liquid ammonia
    
5. Meanwhile, the flashed steam → condensed to **freshwater** using cold seawater
    

Thus, the cycle produces:

### ✔ **Electricity (from the closed cycle)**

### ✔ **Freshwater (from the open cycle)**

---

# ⭐ **WHY USE A HYBRID CYCLE?**

Because:

### ✔ The _open cycle_ produces pure water but has **low efficiency**

### ✔ The _closed cycle_ produces electricity efficiently but does **not produce fresh water**

A hybrid cycle **combines both benefits**.

---

# ⭐ **ADVANTAGES OF HYBRID CYCLE OTEC**

1. Produces both **freshwater** and **electricity**.
    
2. Higher overall efficiency than open cycle alone.
    
3. Reduces turbine erosion (because steam does not drive the turbine).
    
4. Pure water production improves plant economics.
    
5. Lower working fluid requirement compared to standalone closed cycle.
    

---

- Working fluid evaporates & runs turbine
    
- Cold seawater used in condensers
    
- Freshwater collected from condensed steam
