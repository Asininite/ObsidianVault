### **The Dow Fire and Explosion Hazard Index (F&EI)**

#### **1. Introduction and Purpose**
[cite_start]The Dow Fire and Explosion Index is a quantitative method used for ranking the **relative fire and explosion risk** associated with a specific process unit in a chemical plant[cite: 450].
* [cite_start]**Origin:** It was developed by the Dow Chemical Company and is published by the American Institute of Chemical Engineers (AIChE)[cite: 451].
* **Primary Function:** It evaluates the potential risk from a process and assesses the potential loss. [cite_start]It calculates a numerical value (the Index) based on the nature of the process and the properties of the materials involved[cite: 451, 452].
* [cite_start]**Interpretation:** The larger the F&EI value, the more hazardous the process is considered to be[cite: 453].

#### **2. Objectives of the Dow F&EI**
[cite_start]The index serves three main objectives [cite: 454-457]:
1.  **Quantify:** It quantifies the expected damage potential (in realistic terms) that could result from fire or explosion incidents.
2.  **Identify:** It helps identify specific equipment that is likely to contribute to the creation or escalation of an incident.
3.  **Communicate:** It serves as a tool to communicate the fire and explosion potential to design teams and plant personnel.

#### **3. Methodology and Suitability**
* [cite_start]It is specifically tailored for the storage, handling, and processing of explosive and flammable materials in the chemical industry[cite: 459].
* [cite_start]It is designed to be used at the **early stages** of a project (using piping and instrumentation diagrams) as well as for auditing existing plants[cite: 461, 466].
* [cite_start]The final rating provides a relative ranking of hazards and is used to estimate the **damage radius** and financial loss in the event of an accident[cite: 462, 463].

### **4. Calculation Procedure (Step-by-Step)**

The calculation involves a systematic approach using a rating form. [cite_start]The procedure is as follows [cite: 470-475]:



#### **Step 1: Select the Process Unit**
The first step is to identify the "Pertinent Process Unit." [cite_start]This should be the unit that would have the greatest impact on the magnitude of a fire or explosion (e.g., a reactor, storage tank, or distillation column)[cite: 471].

#### **Step 2: Determine the Material Factor (MF)**
This is the foundation of the calculation.
* [cite_start]**Definition:** The Material Factor is a measure of the **intrinsic rate of energy release** from the burning, explosion, or chemical reaction of the substance[cite: 476, 477].
* **Selection:** It is based on the flammability (flash point) and reactivity of the substance. [cite_start]If multiple materials are present, the value for the material with the highest MF is typically used [cite: 479-481].

#### **Step 3: Calculate General Process Hazards ($F_1$)**
[cite_start]These are factors that play a primary role in determining the **magnitude** of the loss following an incident[cite: 483]. You apply penalties for:
* [cite_start]**Exothermic/Endothermic Reactions:** Penalties vary based on the sensitivity of the reaction (e.g., nitration is higher than hydrogenation) [cite: 484-486].
* [cite_start]**Material Handling and Transfer:** Hazards involved in moving materials[cite: 487].
* [cite_start]**Enclosed Units:** Additional hazard if ventilation is restricted (indoor units)[cite: 488].
* [cite_start]**Access:** Penalties if emergency equipment cannot access the unit from at least two sides[cite: 489].
* [cite_start]**Drainage:** Inadequate drainage design that could cause spills to pool near equipment[cite: 490].

#### **Step 4: Calculate Special Process Hazards ($F_2$)**
[cite_start]These are factors known to contribute to the **probability** (likelihood) of an incident involving loss[cite: 492]. Penalties include:
* [cite_start]**Toxic Materials:** Because they make emergency response difficult[cite: 493].
* [cite_start]**Sub-atmospheric Pressure:** Hazard of air leaking into the system[cite: 496].
* [cite_start]**Operation in Flammable Range:** Possibility of air mixing with fuel inside the equipment[cite: 498].
* [cite_start]**Dust Explosion:** Risk varies based on particle size (smaller particles = higher penalty) [cite: 499-501].
* [cite_start]**Relief Pressure & Low Temperature:** Effects of high pressure on leakage rates or brittle fracture of metal at low temps[cite: 502, 506].
* [cite_start]**Quantity of Flammable Material:** Larger quantities = greater potential loss[cite: 507].
* [cite_start]**Corrosion, Leakage, Fired Heaters, Rotating Equipment:** Other specific equipment risks [cite: 509-518].

#### **Step 5: Calculate Unit Hazard Factor ($F_3$)**
[cite_start]The Unit Hazard Factor is calculated by multiplying the General Process Hazards factor by the Special Process Hazards factor[cite: 475].
$$F_3 = F_1 \times F_2$$

#### **Step 6: Calculate the Final Index (F&EI)**
[cite_start]The final Dow Fire and Explosion Index is the product of the Unit Hazard Factor and the Material Factor[cite: 474]:
$$F\&EI = F_3 \times \text{Material Factor (MF)}$$

### **5. Assessment of Hazard Degree**

[cite_start]Once the F&EI is calculated, the degree of hazard is categorized using the following table[cite: 465]:

| F&EI Range | Degree of Hazard |
| :--- | :--- |
| **1 - 60** | Light |
| **61 - 96** | Moderate |
| **97 - 127** | Intermediate |
| **128 - 158** | Heavy |
| **> 159** | Severe |

### **6. Loss Control and Prevention**
The index calculation helps determine what preventative measures are needed. [cite_start]Basic safety measures that should be included in all designs include [cite: 522-539]:
* Adequate water supplies for fire fighting.
* Pressure-relief devices.
* Corrosion-resistant materials.
* Earthing of electrical equipment.
* Safe location of control rooms.
* Adequate drainage for spills.
* Protection of pipe racks and cables from fire.
